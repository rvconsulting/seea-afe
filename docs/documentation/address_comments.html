Proposed text.

Many countries in Eastern and Southern Africa face mounting natural capital stress even where headline growth looks solid. Woody biomass still supplies 78 to 94 percent of national energy in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), Uganda, and Zambia, and that dependence is a documented driver of forest loss. Uganda’s forest cover fell about 60 percent between 1990 and 2015. Zambia lost 1.88 million hectares of tree cover in five years (2018–2023). Agriculture remains the lowest-productivity water user wherever monetary water accounts exist, while taking almost all of the water. Rwanda’s agriculture uses more than 96 percent of freshwater abstraction and generates only 118 Rwf per cubic metre, against 6,236 Rwf/m³ in mining. Uganda’s agriculture generates UGX 1,458/m³ against UGX 364,299/m³ in services. Mauritius loses two-thirds of treated potable water in the pipe network before it reaches households.

This report draws on three sources: the 2025 Global Assessment of Environmental-Economic Accounting, presented to the UN Statistical Commission in 2026 (Committee of Experts on Environmental-Economic Accounting, 2026); a country-by-country review of compiled natural capital accounts (NCAs) for nine of the 26 countries in the World Bank’s Eastern and Southern Africa (AFE) region, namely the DRC, Ethiopia, Kenya, Madagascar, Mauritius, Rwanda, South Africa, Uganda, and Zambia; and World Development Indicators used to place account results next to GDP, sectoral value added, rural population, and CO₂ per capita (World Bank, 2026). The nine countries are a subset of the 26. Three of them (Rwanda, the DRC, and Madagascar) have substantive accounts compiled through World Bank and donor programmes but are not yet registered as formally implementing the System of Environmental-Economic Accounting (SEEA) in the UN classification. “In any form” in this report means that those accounts are reviewed on their merits; it does not mean they are outside the SEEA statistical standard.

Of the 26 AFE countries, 11 are implementing the SEEA. Four are at Stage III, four at Stage II, and three at Stage I under SDG indicator 15.9.1(b). South Africa and Kenya lead on the number of compiled accounts documented in this review. Physical water supply and use tables are the most common entry point.

When account indicators are read against macroeconomic data, three patterns stand out. First, net forest cover change over the full account period—not an annualized rate—is larger where agriculture’s share of GDP is higher, but the relationship is not a law: Uganda and Rwanda each lost about 60 percent of forest cover over 25 years (1990–2015), while Zambia lost 4.5 percent over only five years (2018–2023) despite agriculture contributing just 2.2 percent of GDP. Second, biomass dependence moves inversely with GDP per capita and with conventional CO₂ per capita excluding land use. Countries that look “clean” on energy-sector emissions are often drawing down forest carbon through fuelwood. Third, water and energy productivity gaps between agriculture and other sectors are large in every country that has compiled the relevant supply and use tables. Those gaps are expected at the aggregate sector level because a cubic metre used to grow food cannot match the value added of a cubic metre used in mining or services; their policy value is that they quantify the opportunity cost of allocation, leakage, and irrigation choices.

Thematic accounts add the operational detail that the correlations cannot. Water accounts show that national averages conceal basin-level scarcity (Kenya’s per capita use ranges from 8 m³ to 91 m³ a year) and that unaccounted-for water is a first-order fiscal leak (64.6 percent in Mauritius, 41 percent in Rwanda, 34.8 percent in Uganda). Energy accounts show that biomass dominates physical supply while electricity and petroleum dominate household spending, so tariffs and subsidies are calibrated to a monetized minority of energy use. Land and ecosystem accounts show conversion from woodland to cropland or rangeland, declining dry-season baseflow, and, in the DRC, a global climate-regulation value of forest services on the order of USD 706 billion per year against only about USD 3.3 billion captured domestically.

The most consequential applications are already in government and World Bank decision systems. Kenya’s KENMod macro-fiscal model, fed by NCA data, simulates drought, livestock, and flood shocks and is cited in the 2026 Budget Policy Statement: climate shocks reduce GDP, raise short-term inflation, widen the primary deficit, and increase debt-to-GDP, and they identify sectors that are most exposed (National Treasury of Kenya, 2026). Ethiopia’s Investment Prioritization Tool, built on the same ecosystem-service modelling that underpins the accounts, informed watershed selection for the Climate Action through Landscape Management (CALM) Programme-for-Results, a portfolio of about USD 900 million covering 8,000 community watersheds, and the Ethiopia Strategic Investment Framework for Sustainable Land Management (World Bank BELA Initiative, 2026). Rwanda’s monetary water tables supplied the quantitative case for tariff reform and non-revenue-water reduction. South Africa’s biodiversity tourism accounts put a GDP and jobs number on protected area expansion.

There is a clear opportunity to do more, because the same accounts can inform water allocation and pricing, clean-cooking and grid investment under ASCENT, catchment and restoration targeting, carbon-finance pipelines, and climate-responsive budgeting. Expanding compilation will also position countries for the 2025 System of National Accounts, which treats natural resource depletion as a cost of production and will require SEEA-compatible physical and monetary asset data as an input.

10. Yurani — Expand the SEEA introduction (Central Framework and EA)

Comment. “Expand this paragraph and add one/two more paragraphs with information on the SEEA Central Framework and the SEEA EEA, what it is, how it is developed.”

Location. “The System of Environmental Economic Accounting”

Proposed text.

The System of Environmental-Economic Accounting (SEEA) (European Commission et al., 2013) is the international statistical standard for measuring the environment and its relationship with the economy. It is maintained by the UN Committee of Experts on Environmental-Economic Accounting (UNCEEA) under the UN Statistical Commission, in the same institutional family as the System of National Accounts. The 2025 Global Assessment of SEEA Implementation (Committee of Experts on Environmental-Economic Accounting, 2026) provides the global context for the AFE region’s progress. This report supplements that assessment with an individual review of all 26 AFE countries to determine whether any form of NCA has been undertaken, regardless of formal SEEA status. The review examines whether each of 36 distinct SEEA accounts has been compiled at least once.

The SEEA has two complementary parts. The SEEA Central Framework (SEEA CF), adopted as an international statistical standard in 2012, accounts for individual environmental assets (such as water, energy, timber, minerals, and land) and for physical and monetary flows between the environment and the economy. Its core building blocks are physical supply and use tables, monetary supply and use tables, and asset accounts. These tables are designed to be internally consistent with the SNA, so that, for example, water or energy used by a sector can be read against that sector’s value added.

The SEEA Ecosystem Accounting (SEEA EA), adopted by the Statistical Commission in 2021, accounts for ecosystems as spatial assets. It records extent (how much of each ecosystem type exists), condition (the quality of those ecosystems), ecosystem services in physical terms, and, where possible, monetary values and ecosystem-asset values. Thematic accounts (forests, oceans, protected areas, and related topics) sit alongside both frameworks and apply the same accounting rules to a policy domain. Accounts are typically developed by national statistical offices working with line ministries, using existing statistical, administrative, and geospatial data, and they are compiled for a country as a whole even when they are also disaggregated spatially for analysis.

Proposed text.

Water Supply and Use Tables from Rwanda, Uganda, and Mauritius provide a direct measure of how efficiently different economic sectors convert water into economic value. The gaps are large. In Uganda, the services sector generates about 250 times more economic value per cubic metre than agriculture. In Mauritius, manufacturing and services generate about 214 times more. In Rwanda, services generate about 89 times more and mining about 53 times more than agriculture.

That ranking is a common way to present monetary water accounts, and it should not be read as a surprise or as an argument that agriculture is an inefficient use of water in the abstract. Value added per cubic metre will almost always be lower in agriculture than in mining or services, whether or not water is the binding constraint. The comparison becomes policy-relevant when it is paired with two other facts from the same tables: agriculture’s share of water, and the direction of change where a time series exists.

On shares, agriculture absorbs the overwhelming majority of water in all three countries. In Mauritius it represents only 4 percent of GDP but 302 of the 344 million cubic metres abstracted from the environment each year. On change over time, the accounts do not show agricultural water productivity collapsing. Rwanda’s national water-use efficiency rose from about 4,500 Rwf/m³ in 2012 to 4,762 Rwf/m³ in 2015 (constant 2014 prices), while GDP grew 25 percent and total water use grew only 10 percent (Government of Rwanda, 2019). Uganda’s national water productivity rose from UGX 3,014/m³ in 2015 to UGX 5,557/m³ in 2024, an 84 percent increase, even though agriculture remains the least productive sector at UGX 1,458/m³ (Uganda Bureau of Statistics, 2025). Mauritius’s ten-year physical series (2013–2022) shows agricultural abstraction stable at about 300–304 Mm³ while unaccounted-for water in the utility network worsened from 56.4 to 64.6 percent (Statistics Mauritius, 2024).

The implication is therefore not to move water out of food production on the basis of a single-year ratio. It is to use the accounts to track whether agricultural returns per cubic metre are rising, and to target irrigation efficiency, crop diversification, and leak reduction where they are not. E. Balance SEEA’s national purpose with spatial disaggregation

Comment. The SEEA Central Framework 2012 states: “The main focus of the accounting descriptions and explanations is on accounting for a country as a whole. This aligns with the intent of the SNA and with the general purpose of the Central Framework to be a national accounting tool rather than used for accounting at the level of an economic unit.” One of the most successful applications is Ethiopia’s IPT at grid and watershed level, which does not align with that main objective.

Location. “Way forward” / sub-national disaggregation recommendation

Proposed text.

Keep national accounts as the statistical objective, and treat spatial layers as an analytical extension—not a substitute. The SEEA Central Framework is explicit that its main focus is accounting for a country as a whole, in line with the SNA, and that it is a national accounting tool rather than a tool for accounting at the level of an economic unit (European Commission et al., 2013). That objective should remain the test of whether a country is implementing SEEA: can the NSO publish national supply, use, and asset tables that reconcile with the national accounts?

Some of the most successful applications in this review use a finer grain. Ethiopia’s Investment Prioritization Tool uses ecosystem-service modelling at grid and watershed level to inform CALM I and II and the national SLM investment framework (World Bank BELA Initiative, 2026). Kenya’s water tables are compiled by basin. South Africa’s water accounts are built around 22 Strategic Water Source Areas. Those products are consistent with SEEA concepts (extent, condition, services, and physical flows), but they are not, on their own, the Central Framework’s national account. The distinction matters for what gets funded. If spatial tools replace national tables, countries will have excellent maps and still be unable to put depletion in the SNA or to report SDG 15.9.1(b). If national tables are compiled without any spatial split, they will miss the targeting use that made the Ethiopia IPT bankable.

The practical recommendation is therefore a two-layer programme. First, a national SEEA compilation hosted in the statistical office, covering the accounts that SNA 2025 and GBF reporting will require. Second, where the biophysical modelling already exists, a spatial disaggregation that is reconciled to the national totals—so that watershed priorities sum to the national service supply, and basin water use sums to the national SUT. Ethiopia’s IPT should be presented as evidence that the modelling behind the accounts can inform World Bank operations at watershed scale, not as evidence that SEEA has become a project-appraisal tool in place of a national account.

F. Time series are the most useful product and the hardest to sustain

Comment. “Time series data would be the most useful, but this is hard to do in practice. Consistency with which source data are compiled over time, changes in the classifications, coverage, and definitions used in the collection of source data can require significant re-working to ensure consistency and quality. This would be challenging in practice when data are compiled on an irregular basis as it is currently done in most AFE countries.”

Location. “Way forward” / institutionalizing accounts

Proposed text.

Treat a short, consistent time series as more valuable than a single comprehensive snapshot, and budget for the re-working that consistency requires. Almost every application in this report is a trend argument: Uganda’s water productivity rising from 2015 to 2024; Rwanda’s decoupling between 2012 and 2015; DRC forest depletion accelerating between 2010 and 2020; Mauritius unaccounted-for water rising from 2013 to 2022. Cross-section comparisons across countries are weaker, because account windows, forest definitions, and land-cover legends differ.

Building those series is harder than compiling a first account. Source data change classification, coverage, and definitions. Ethiopia’s land accounts already caution that a recorded forest-cover gain may partly be a change of source and definition (Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia, 2026). Zambia’s MODIS legend is not the national land-cover legend. When compilation is irregular, as it still is in most AFE countries, each new round can become a new baseline rather than a new observation. Restoring comparability then requires back-casting, bridge tables, and explicit overlap years—work that is technical, unglamorous, and rarely funded by a project that wants a new account type.

The implication for support is to finance reconciliation across vintages as a deliverable: a documented class mapping, a revised back series, and a quality note. Mauritius’s fifth water-account edition and Uganda’s tenth water-account iteration are the regional proof that this is possible when the same institution repeats the same tables. Forest, land, and ecosystem accounts in the region are still mostly one-off compilations, and they will not become time series without that institutional repetition.

G. Quality checks: same accounting period for physical and monetary data

Comment. “Something to say on quality checks and consistency: It is important to ensure that the source data underpinning the physical and the monetary data relate to the same accounting period. Generally, monetary accounts will be compiled on a financial or calendar year basis. Physical data may be compiled on a basis that aligns more closely to natural environmental patterns and seasons. Where needed, adjustments to take these differences into account may need to be made.”

Location. “Common Data Gaps and Systemic Patterns” or “Way forward”

Proposed text.

Align physical and monetary source data on the same accounting period, and record the adjustments when that is not possible. A recurring quality risk in this review is that physical and monetary numbers that appear in the same table do not always describe the same twelve months. Monetary accounts follow the financial or calendar year used in the SNA. Physical data often follow hydrological years, growing seasons, satellite composites, or the year a survey happened to be fielded. Kenya’s monetary energy SUT is for 2024; the physical energy balance it is read against is compiled on a different production calendar. Ethiopia’s land-cover years are 2013 and 2022; the GDP and agricultural-GDP damage figures used alongside them are not always for those same years. Forest-cover change in ?@fig-forest-agr is a multi-year stock change; agricultural value added is a single-year flow.

Where the periods do not coincide, compilers should either (i) allocate physical flows to the SNA year using within-year production profiles (for example, monthly hydropower generation or seasonal crop calendars), or (ii) publish the physical year and the monetary year side by side and avoid computing ratios that mix them. The second option is a minimum quality check for every monetary supply and use table in the region. Without it, water productivity, energy productivity, and ecosystem-service shares of GDP can move because the calendar moved, not because the economy or the environment did.

H. Machine learning for gap imputation when each country has few observations

Comment. “Recommendation on Use of AI: Machine learning for gap imputation… Please elaborate on what methodologies/econometric or machine learning methods could be used for inputting data, given the few data points available for each account and country.”

Location. “Leveraging Artificial Intelligence” / machine-learning bullet

Note. Deep learning is the wrong family of methods here. The constraint is small n (a handful of years per country, nine countries with accounts), not unstructured imagery. Imagery is the right place for deep learning (item 37). Imputation of account cells needs regularized, hierarchical, and survey-statistical methods.

Proposed text.

  • Machine learning and statistical imputation for sparse account tables. Most AFE countries have one or two compiled years per account, and some tables have entire rows missing (groundwater, informal biomass, non-market services). With so few observations, large neural nets will overfit. The useful methods are those designed for small n, hierarchical pooling, and honest uncertainty.

    Borrow strength across countries, not only across years. Mixed-effects or hierarchical Bayesian models treat each country as a draw from a regional distribution of, for example, agricultural water-use intensity or biomass share of household energy. Uganda’s and Rwanda’s water productivity, and DRC’s, Uganda’s, and Zambia’s biomass shares, then become priors for a country that has not yet compiled the table. Predictive mean matching in multiple imputation (MICE) is a related, simpler option: a missing cell is filled with a donor value from a country-year with a similar GDP per capita, rural population share, and forest cover, and the imputation is repeated to produce an interval rather than a single number.

    Use global geospatial and FAO/AQUASTAT series as covariates, then calibrate to the national account. Where a country has one good SEEA year, that year can pin a mass-imputation or small-area model that predicts other years from satellite land cover, rainfall, night lights, and published FAO water or energy balances. The national account remains the benchmark; the model fills gaps and is not allowed to overwrite a compiled cell. Elastic-net or ridge regression is appropriate when the number of covariates approaches the number of compiled country-years. Quantile random forests can supply prediction intervals, but they should be run in a leave-one-country-out loop so that performance is not judged on the same points used to train.

    Do not impute what the accounts are meant to reveal. Informal charcoal, unmetered groundwater, and unpriced household water are structurally missing, not randomly missing. Imputing them from formal-sector ratios will reproduce the bias the accounts exist to correct. For those cells, the honest products are (i) a range from a hierarchical model with a wide prior, or (ii) a flag that the cell is unmeasured. Recent work on conformal prediction and on Bayesian measurement-error models is useful here because it attaches a coverage guarantee to the gap rather than a false point estimate.

    Governance of imputed cells. Every imputed figure in a published table should be marked, with the method, the donor years or countries, and the interval. Otherwise an imputed water-productivity ratio will be read as if it had been compiled from a supply and use table. The purpose of these methods is to let a country publish a first complete-looking framework and a revision plan, not to replace compilation.
